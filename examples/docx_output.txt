Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) are widely recognized for their effectiveness in solving complex optimization problems, particularly when the underlying objective function is unknown, non-differentiable, or computationally expensive to evaluate. Their success stems from a population-based search strategy that iteratively refines candidate solutions through variation operators such as mutation and recombination. However, conventional EAs depend heavily on manually designed operators and heuristics tailored to specific domains. The need for domain expertise in configuring these operators represents a major bottleneck, limiting the adaptability of these algorithms across unfamiliar or dynamically changing optimization landscapes ( Liu et al., 2024; He and Liu, 2024 ). Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened new opportunities for automating the design of optimization algorithms. LLMs have demonstrated a remarkable capacity for reasoning about code and synthesizing algorithmic logic through natural language instructions. Frameworks like the Large Language Model Evolutionary Algorithm ( LLaMEA ) utilize iterative prompting to enable a language model to generate, evaluate, and refine candidate optimization heuristics based on evaluation metrics and runtime diagnostics ( van Stein and Bäck, 2024 ). Despite producing competitive heuristics, existing LLM-driven frameworks suffer from two critical structural limitations. First, they are largely mutation-centric . They operate by maintaining a single best-performing algorithm and repeatedly prompting the model to modify it. While effective for local search, this lacks the ability to recombine complementary features from multiple candidate solutions. Early attempts to integrate crossover often relied on direct text splicing, leading to "semantic blindness", which leads to syntactically incorrect or logically inconsistent offspring programs ( Meyerson et al., 2023 ). Second, many frameworks rely on static operator schedules. Optimization is inherently a non-stationary process; exploration-oriented strategies are essential early on, while exploitation becomes critical later. Static operator probabilities cannot adapt to these changing requirements, often resulting in stagnation. To address these limitations, this study proposes GA- LLaMEA (Generative Adaptive Large Language Model Evolutionary Algorithm). This novel framework introduces an Inspiration-Based Generative Crossover mechanism, drawing inspiration from recent work on semantic recombination in LLM-driven evolutionary systems such as CODEEVOLVE (Assumpção et al., 2026), and leverages LLM reasoning to transfer conceptual algorithmic strategies between parent solutions, synthesizing coherent offspring without blind code concatenation. Furthermore, GA- LLaMEA employs Discounted Thompson Sampling, a probabilistic Multi-Armed Bandit algorithm, as an adaptive controller for operator selection ( Qi et al., 2023 ). By dynamically adjusting the probability of selecting different generative operators based on recent performance feedback, the framework autonomously balances exploration and exploitation. Through this generative-adaptive approach, GA- LLaMEA aims to overcome the structural rigidity of current LLM-driven systems and enhance the robustness of automated algorithm design in complex black-box optimization environments. Background of the Study The optimization of complex systems, ranging from logistical routing to neural network hyperparameter tuning, remains a fundamental challenge in computer science. These tasks are frequently treated as black-box optimization problems because the internal structure of the objective function is unavailable. For decades, traditional EAs have served as the standard approach for navigating these large search spaces without requiring gradient information ( Hansen et al., 2021 ). However, the efficacy of traditional EAs is constrained by the manual configuration of variation operators. A strategy that performs optimally in one problem domain may fail entirely in another. This reliance on human intuition has driven the field of Automated Algorithm Design (AAD), which seeks to systematically automate the creation and improvement of optimization heuristics ( Liu et al., 2024 ). The integration of Large Language Models has recently introduced a paradigm shift in AAD. Modern language models act as generative agents, proposing and refining algorithms through iterative evaluation loops. Frameworks such as Evolution of Heuristics ( EoH ) and LLaMEA have successfully demonstrated that LLMs can automatically generate optimization algorithms that rival expert-designed baselines (Liu et al., 2024; van Stein and Bäck, 2024) . As this field matures, the architectural requirements for these LLM driven optimizers are evolving. While foundational systems like the baseline LLaMEA framework support population based evolution maintaining a diverse pool of candidate algorithms they remain fundamentally mutation centric. Because the baseline architecture lacks a native crossover operator, candidates are iteratively refined in isolation. This prevents the system from executing global exploration by recombining complementary algorithmic strategies discovered by different individuals in the population. More recent advancements, such as EoH , CODEEVOLVE, and LLaMEA BO, have demonstrated the immense value of integrating crossover (Li et al., 2025; Assumpção et al., 2026). However, introducing multiple variation operators (mutation and crossover) creates a new challenge: managing their deployment. Optimization landscapes are rarely static, demanding an algorithmic architecture capable of dynamic adaptation. By unifying LLM-driven algorithm synthesis with the adaptive decision-making of Discounted Thompson Sampling, GA- LLaMEA represents the next progression in AAD. This research builds upon the foundational success of generative heuristics by transitioning from rigid, mutation-heavy refinement toward a dynamic, context-aware evolutionary search. 1.1 Statement of the Problem Recent developments in Large Language Models have enabled new approaches for automated algorithm design and heuristic generation. By integrating language models with evolutionary computation, several frameworks have demonstrated the potential to automatically generate optimization algorithms through iterative evaluation and feedback driven improvement. Systems such as Evolution of Heuristics and Reflective Evolution illustrate how Large Language Models can function as generative agents capable of producing and refining candidate heuristics within evolutionary search processes ( Liu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024 ). Similarly, algorithm discovery frameworks such as FunSearch and CODEEVOLVE further demonstrate how language model reasoning can be integrated into evolutionary pipelines to automate the discovery of computational solutions ( Ellenberg et al., 2025; Assumpção et al., 2026 ). Recent studies analysing LLM-driven meta-heuristic discovery further demonstrate that these systems exhibit complex behavioural dynamics during the evolutionary process. Behaviour space analysis of algorithms generated by LLaMEA reveals that different prompt strategies and mutation configurations can lead to significantly different exploration and exploitation patterns, as well as varying convergence behaviour across optimization landscapes ( van Stein et al., 2025) . These findings highlight the importance of understanding and controlling the algorithm generation process in LLM-driven evolutionary systems to ensure stable optimization performance. Despite these advancements, several structural limitations remain in current LLM driven evolutionary optimization frameworks. Many existing systems emphasize mutation based refinement while lacking mechanisms for effective recombination and adaptive operator control. These limitations restrict the ability of optimization systems to explore diverse algorithmic strategies and adapt to dynamic optimization landscapes. Specifically, this study addresses the following research problems: Underutilized Populations (The Mutation Centric Limitation). While frameworks like baseline LLaMEA support population based search, they rely primarily on mutation based refinement. Candidates in the population are improved through incremental, isolated modifications generated by the language model. Because there is no mechanism to recombine complementary strategies from multiple candidate solutions, the evolutionary search is constrained to parallel silos, often leading to premature convergence. Ineffective Recombination Mechanisms. Although contemporary population based frameworks have introduced crossover, the method of recombination is critical. Performing crossover purely at the syntactic level through direct code concatenation or text level splicing frequently produces invalid or logically inconsistent programs because it ignores the semantic structure of the algorithms. To effectively modernize the LLaMEA architecture, there is a need for robust, generative mechanisms capable of synthesizing algorithmic strategies in a coherent and executable manner, rather than relying on blind text merging. Lack of Adaptive Operator Selection. With the necessary introduction of generative crossover to population based LLM frameworks, a critical secondary challenge emerges: operator scheduling. Traditional evolutionary systems frequently rely on static schedules to dictate when to apply mutation versus recombination. However, optimization landscapes are inherently non-stationary; the relative effectiveness of exploration and exploitation fluctuates continuously throughout the search process. While adaptive operator selection has been recognized as a vital mechanism to manage these dynamics (Aydin et al., 2023), current LLM driven frameworks largely lack these sophisticated controls. Consequently, relying on rigid, static probabilities inevitably leads to misaligned search behavior, causing inefficient exploration during early phases or premature stagnation in later stages. Given these limitations, there is a need for a generative adaptive evolutionary framework that can integrate conceptual recombination mechanisms with adaptive operator selection strategies to enhance the performance and robustness of LLM driven optimization systems. Thus, this study seeks to develop a framework that addresses these limitations by introducing generative crossover and adaptive operator selection using modified bandit based control mechanisms to improve algorithm discovery in black box optimization environments. 1.2 Significance of the Study This research advances Evolutionary Computation by operationalizing GA- LLaMEA , shifting the paradigm of LLM-based optimization from static code refinement toward adaptive algorithm synthesis. By integrating adaptive operator selection mechanisms with Large Language Models, this study provides a framework for optimization systems that dynamically regulate their search strategies during the evolutionary process. The significance of this study is defined by three key contributions: Modernizing LLaMEA with Generative Algorithm Synthesis This study advances the foundational LLaMEA framework by introducing a Generative Crossover mechanism. Instead of relying on rigid text splicing, the language model synthesizes new algorithms by integrating conceptual strategies from multiple parent solutions. This brings LLaMEA's explorative capabilities in line with state of the art population frameworks while ensuring structurally coherent and executable algorithms. This approach leverages the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models to generate structurally coherent and executable algorithms, advancing the field of automated algorithm design and compositional evolution ( Meyerson et al., 2023; Elsken et al., 2020 ). Advancing Adaptive Optimization through Bandit Based Control While other frameworks possess multiple variation operators, this study pioneers the integration of an adaptive decision making mechanism into LLM driven evolutionary optimization. By employing a modified bandit based operator selection strategy derived from Discounted Thompson Sampling (D TS), the GA LLaMEA framework dynamically regulates the probability of selecting mutation versus generative crossover based on real time performance feedback, eliminating the need for fragile static schedules. Establishing Rigorous Empirical Standards for LLM Driven Optimizers This study also contributes methodological rigor to the evaluation of LLM driven optimization systems by validating generated algorithms using the MA-BBOB benchmarking framework. Benchmarking frameworks for automated algorithm discovery emphasize the importance of standardized problem suites, performance metrics, and experiment logging in order to ensure reproducible evaluation and fair comparison of optimization algorithms ( van Stein et al., 2025 ). By utilizing established benchmarking practices and performance metrics such as Area Over the Convergence Curve (AOCC), this study contributes to the development of more rigorous evaluation methodologies for LLM driven optimization systems. 1.3 Objectives of the Study To address the structural deficits outlined above, this study aims to develop a generative adaptive evolutionary framework that improves the capability of Large Language Model driven optimization systems to explore and synthesize effective algorithms. Specifically, the objectives of this study are: Develop the GA- LLaMEA Framework Design and implement a generative adaptive evolutionary framework that integrates a modified bandit based operator selection policy derived from Discounted Thompson Sampling to dynamically regulate the balance between exploration and exploitation during the optimization process. Bandit algorithms provide an effective mechanism for sequential decision making in dynamic environments, allowing systems to adapt operator selection strategies based on observed performance feedback ( Qi et al., 2023 ). Implement Generative Crossover Mechanisms Implement Generative Crossover Mechanisms Upgrade the baseline LLaMEA architecture by developing a Generative Crossover mechanism that leverages the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. This will enable the synthesis of coherent offspring algorithms by combining conceptual strategies from multiple parent solutions, aligning the framework with modern population based AAD standards. Validate the Framework using MA-BBOB Benchmarking Evaluate the performance of the proposed framework by benchmarking the generated algorithms using the Many-Affine Black-Box Optimization Benchmark (MA-BBOB) function generator. The MA-BBOB framework extends the classical BBOB benchmark suite by generating diverse optimization landscapes through affine combinations of existing benchmark functions, enabling more comprehensive evaluation of optimization algorithms across a wider range of problem characteristics ( Vermetten et al., 2023 ). 1.4 Scope and Limitation Scope This study focuses on the development and validation of the GA- LLaMEA framework, specifically targeted at single-objective, continuous black-box optimization. The research utilizes the Black-Box Optimization Benchmark (BBOB) suite, comprising 24 distinct separable, ill-conditioned, and multimodal noiseless functions, as the standardized environment for evaluating algorithm generalizability. The generative component employs Gemini-2.5-flash accessed via API, leveraging their advanced reasoning for iterative feedback loops, while all resulting heuristics are implemented in Python to ensure compatibility with the COCO platform. A central aspect of this scope is the assessment of Discounted Thompson Sampling (DTS) as a high-level adaptive controller, specifically analyzing the influence of the discount factor (γ) on managing the trade-offs between Mutation, Refinement, and Crossover operators. Limitation The boundaries of this research exclude the application of model fine-tuning or weight retraining; the methodology is strictly delimited to In-Context Learning (ICL) and prompt engineering to isolate the efficacy of the evolutionary architecture rather than the model's learning capacity. Furthermore, the study does not address combinatorial optimization, large-scale global optimization (LSGO) exceeding 100 dimensions, or multi-objective problems requiring Pareto-front analysis. These exclusions are established to maintain a feasible computational budget regarding API costs and latency, and to ensure a focused analysis on solution quality. Chapter II REVIEW OF LITERATURE This section synthesizes the theoretical foundations of the study, examining the intersection of Large Language Models (LLMs), Automated Algorithm Design (AAD), and Adaptive Control Theory. It frames the research gap by contrasting current static, mutation-centric frameworks with the proposed generative-adaptive approach. 2.1 Theoretical Foundations of Evolutionary Computation The theoretical underpinnings of this study are rooted in the fundamental principles of Evolutionary Computation (EC), specifically the mechanisms governing search dynamics, information exchange, and the balance between exploration and exploitation. Optimization in high-dimensional landscapes is fundamentally a decision-making process under uncertainty, characterized by the Exploration-Exploitation Dilemma. As formulated by March (1991) and later adapted to evolutionary systems by Holland (1992), an effective optimizer must balance exploration, the acquisition of new information about the search space, and exploitation, the refinement of known high-quality solutions. Static algorithms typically fail to manage this trade-off effectively; for instance, a purely explorative algorithm wastes resources, while a purely exploitative one converges prematurely. Recent empirical work further reinforces this limitation. Yin et al. (2024) conducted a rigorous analysis of mutation operators in LLM-driven systems, showing that static parameter configurations are insufficient and that optimal performance requires adaptive mechanisms capable of responding to changing search dynamics in real time. 2.2 No free lunch theorems Finally, the necessity for Automated Algorithm Design is justified by the No Free Lunch (NFL) theorems (Wolpert and Macready, 1997). The NFL theorems state that averaged over all possible problem classes, no single optimization algorithm outperforms any other. This implies that the "universal" heuristic does not exist; effective optimization requires tailoring the algorithm to the specific structure of the problem instance. This theoretical boundary motivates the shift from designing heuristics (manual engineering) to evolving heuristics (hyper-heuristics). It suggests that the goal of this research is not to generate a single "super-algorithm," but to develop a generative framework capable of adapting its output to the idiosyncrasies of the target domain, a capability uniquely suited to the broad knowledge base of Large Language Models. The "No Free Lunch" theorems continue to drive research into automated design. Tan et al. (2025) frame this in their new taxonomy of "Meta-Black-Box-Optimization" ( MetaBBO ). They argue that because no single heuristic works for all problems, the future of optimization lies not in designing better static algorithms, but in designing systems that can generate algorithms on the fly. This shift from "Algorithm Selection" to "Algorithm Generation" defines the current state of the art. 2.3 The building block hypothesis and recombination A central tenet of Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) is the role of recombination (crossover) in synthesizing novel solutions. Holland's Schema Theorem (1975) provides the mathematical justification for this mechanism, positing that short, low-order, highly fit subsets of the genome (termed "schemata" or "building blocks") grow exponentially in frequency over generations. The Building Block Hypothesis (Goldberg, 1989) extends this, arguing that complex optimal solutions emerge through the hierarchical combination of these smaller building blocks. For example, in the domain of heuristic generation, one parent algorithm may possess an efficient selection operator, while another contains a superior update rule. Mutation alone is unlikely to merge these two distinct features, as it would require a precise sequence of random modifications to reconstruct one feature within the other. In contrast, crossover enables the direct recombination of these semantic components. Opris et al. (2025) revisited this theory through rigorous runtime analysis, demonstrating that on many objective landscapes, crossover-enabled algorithms achieve polynomial-time convergence, whereas mutation-only baselines require exponential time. This theoretical gap highlights a critical deficiency in current LLM-based optimizers, which remain predominantly mutation-centric (Yin et al., 2024). The efficacy of recombination is further validated by recent advancements in LLaMEA -BO, a framework for automatically generating Bayesian Optimization (BO) algorithms. Li et al. (2025) demonstrate that while the original mutation-only (1+1) Evolution Strategy consistently underperformed, transitioning to a population-based approach with explicit crossover operators enabled the LLM to effectively combine algorithmic components. Because BO algorithms are inherently modular, comprising distinct components such as initial design strategies, surrogate models, and acquisition functions, they are particularly well-suited for semantic recombination. By prompting the LLM to analyze and integrate the logic of two parent algorithms, LLaMEA -BO successfully generated novel solvers, such as TREvol , which emerged from a crossover operation and significantly outperformed state-of-the-art baselines. Collectively, these findings support the shift toward compositional generalization. While Jiang et al. (2024) observe that LLMs inherently exhibit compositional reasoning capabilities, LLaMEA -BO provides empirical evidence that this capability can be operationalized for valid crossover. Rather than relying on syntactic text splicing, LLMs can synthesize coherent offspring by recombining high-level algorithmic logic, thereby validating the Building Block Hypothesis in the context of code evolution. 2.4 The Evolution of Automated Algorithm Design (AAD) The trajectory of AAD has accelerated rapidly, moving from rigid tree-based evolution to flexible, natural-language-driven synthesis. Tan et al. (2025) classify this evolution into three distinct phases. 2.4.1 Genetic programming and symbolic regression The first era of AAD was dominated by Genetic Programming (GP), popularized by Koza (1992). GP evolves computer programs by representing them as syntax trees, where internal nodes represent functions (e.g., +, *, if) and leaf nodes represent terminals (variables, constants). While GP achieved notable successes, such as evolving antenna designs and quantum logic circuits, it suffered from severe structural limitations. The most prominent was "code bloat"— the tendency of evolved programs to grow uncontrollably in size without a corresponding increase in fitness (Koza, 1992; Angeline, 1997). Bloated trees are computationally expensive to evaluate and, crucially, impossible for humans to interpret. Furthermore, GP requires a rigorous definition of the function set and terminal set, limiting its flexibility. If a required mathematical operator was not pre-defined by the human engineer, the GP could not "invent" it, restricting the search to a closed universe of primitives. 2.4.2 Hyper-heuristics: selection vs. generation To overcome the fragility of low-level GP, the field moved toward Hyper-heuristics defined as "heuristics to choose heuristics" (Burke et al., 2013). Drake et al. (2020) categorize these approaches into two classes: Selection Hyper-heuristics: These methods manage a pool of pre-existing low-level heuristics (e.g., 2-opt, shift, swap) and use a high-level strategy (often Reinforcement Learning) to select the best operator for the current state of the problem. While robust, they are limited by the quality of the pre-defined pool. Generation Hyper-heuristics: These methods typically use GP to construct new heuristics from components. While more powerful, they inherited the interpretability issues of GP. This era highlighted the "Interpretability Gap." Although machines could optimize algorithms, the resulting code was often a "black box" of spaghetti logic, preventing human experts from learning why a generated algorithm worked. 2.5 Large Language Models as Optimization Agents The third and current era of AAD is defined by the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), which bridge the gap between automated synthesis and human readability. 2.5.1 Emergent reasoning and in-context learning Unlike GP, which manipulates syntax trees, LLMs manipulate semantic tokens based on a vast pre-trained corpora of human-written code . This allows them to leverage "In-Context Learning" (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020), where the model learns to perform a task simply by observing examples in the prompt, without weight updates. Recent work suggests that LLMs possess emergent reasoning capabilities that resemble an evolutionary search process. When prompted with a "Chain of Thought" (Wei et al., 2022), LLMs can decompose complex algorithmic problems into intermediate logical steps. In the context of AAD, this means an LLM does not just output code; it can explain the logic behind a proposed modification, effectively solving the interpretability crisis of the GP era. 2.5.2 State-of-the-art frameworks Several high-profile frameworks have recently validated LLMs as optimization agents. FunSearch (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024): Published in Nature, FunSearch (Function Search) pairs an LLM with a systematic evaluator to solve hard combinatorial problems like the Cap Set problem. It utilizes a "best-so-far" prompting strategy, effectively performing a local hill-climbing search in the space of Python programs. Eureka (Ma et al., 2024): In the domain of Reinforcement Learning, Eureka utilizes GPT-4 to write reward functions for robotic control. It introduces an "evolutionary reflection" mechanism, where the LLM analyzes the failure logs of previous attempts to generate improved code. Ma et al. report that Eureka outperforms expert human-designed rewards on 83% of benchmark tasks. Evolution of Heuristics ( EoH ) (Liu et al., 2024): EoH introduces a dual-population strategy, evolving both the Python code and the natural language "thought" or description associated with it. This creates a semantic bridge, ensuring that code changes are driven by conceptual reasoning rather than random token flipping. LLaMEA and LLaMEA -BO: The original LLaMEA (van Stein and Back, 2024) established a feedback-driven cycle where an LLM iteratively refines algorithms based on runtime error logs and performance metrics. This framework originally adopted a (1+1) evolution strategy to keep computational costs low and ensure interpretable progress. However, recognizing the limitations of single-parent evolution, extensions such as LLaMEA -BO have recently introduced population-based dynamics with explicit crossover. This shift is justified by the observation that code components in domains like Bayesian Optimization are highly composable, making them suitable for recombination. 2.6 The Deficit of Mutation-Centric Search Despite the rapid advancement of AAD, many LLM driven frameworks fail to fully leverage their architectural capabilities. For instance, while the baseline LLaMEA framework supports population based evolution allowing for the concurrent evaluation of multiple candidate algorithms its standard workflow remains structurally biased toward mutation. Because it lacks a dedicated crossover operator, candidates evolve in parallel silos. This mutation centric approach restricts the search to the local basins of attraction of the individual seeds. Opris et al. (2025) demonstrate that this limitation is fundamental; rigorous runtime analysis reveals an exponential performance gap between mutation only and crossover enabled algorithms. Specifically, without crossover to share information across the population, algorithms may require exponential time to converge in many objective landscapes, whereas crossover enables polynomial time convergence by recombining distinct building blocks discovered by different individuals. Recent literature has actively addressed this limitation. Frameworks such as Evolution of Heuristics ( EoH ) (Liu et al., 2024), CODEEVOLVE (Assumpção et al., 2026), and LLaMEA BO (Li et al., 2025) have successfully activated the full potential of their populations by utilizing explicit crossover. However, the successful integration of crossover in these contemporary models introduces a secondary problem that remains largely unsolved in the AAD literature: ensuring the semantic validity of the offspring and dynamically scheduling these diverse operators in non-stationary optimization environments. 2.7 From Syntactic Splicing to Generative Crossover Integrating crossover into LLM-based evolution presents a challenge: ensuring the semantic integrity of the offspring. Syntactic Blindness: Early approaches, such as Language Model Crossover (LMX) (Meyerson et al., 2023), treat algorithms as text strings, prompting the LLM to concatenate or blend parent strings (e.g., "Combine Parent A and Parent B"). Critics argue this leads to "semantic blindness", where the LLM merges text patterns without understanding the functional logic, resulting in high rates of invalid or incoherent code (Yao et al., 2025). 2.7.2 Generative Synthesis To address this, recent literature proposes Generative Crossover. Instead of text splicing, the LLM is prompted to conceptually synthesize a new solution that inherits specific logical behaviors (e.g., "Selection Strategy" from Parent A, "Update Rule" from Parent B) (Wang et al., 2025). Early approaches such as Evolution of Heuristics ( EoH ) (Liu et al., 2024) implement crossover-like mechanisms through exploration prompt strategies, where multiple parent heuristics are analyzed and recombined at the level of high-level ideas to generate new candidates. More recent frameworks such as CODEEVOLVE (Assumpção et al., 2026) further operationalize this concept through inspiration-based crossover, where large language models explicitly integrate patterns, logic, and functional components from multiple parent solutions during generation. This distinction between syntactic splicing and semantically guided synthesis is central to the proposed GA- LLaMEA framework, which aims to maximize the viability of recombinant offspring. 2.8 Adaptive Operator Selection in Non-Stationary Environments Optimization is an inherently non-stationary process; the effectiveness of operators changes over time. Exploration (crossover) is vital in early stages, while exploitation (mutation) is preferred in later stages. 2.8.1 Reinforcement learning for control: the bandit approach To automate the selection of operators, recent literature has turned to Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically the Multi Armed Bandit (MAB) formulation. However, standard algorithms like Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) and standard Thompson Sampling (TS) rely on the assumption of stationarity, specifically that the underlying reward distribution of an arm remains constant over time. This assumption is critically violated in evolutionary computation, where an operator that is highly effective in the early stages, such as Random Initialization, often yields diminishing returns in later stages. To address this specific failure mode, Qi et al. (2023) proposed Discounted Thompson Sampling (DTS), a variant that introduces a tunable discount factor to reduce the weight of historical data. Crucially, this method condenses the complex problem of adaptive control into a single hyperparameter, where the discount factor governs the effective memory length of the system. A lower value allows for rapid adaptation in highly volatile landscapes by quickly discarding old information, while a higher value favors stability in smoother environments. Unlike standard TS, which accumulates "regret" linearly in switching environments by overvaluing historical success, properly tuning the discount factor allows DTS to enforce a specific "fading memory" rate suited to the problem's dynamics. This enables the policy to detect environmental change points, such as the necessary shift from exploration to exploitation, significantly faster than sliding window approaches. Consequently, DTS offers a robust theoretical framework that replaces the need for manual operator scheduling with a single, interpretable control for non-stationarity. 2.9 Search Landscape Analysis and Behavioral Diversity 2.9.1 Deception and the fitness trap Optimization landscapes often contained deceptive regions that misled search algorithms toward suboptimal solutions. Mutation-centric approaches were particularly vulnerable to these traps, as they relied on incremental improvements that reinforced local optima. Recombination provided a mechanism for escaping these regions by enabling discontinuous jumps in the search space. By combining distinct algorithmic components, crossover facilitated exploration beyond local basins of attraction. 2.9.2 Behavioral diversity and search trajectories Recent studies emphasized the importance of behavioral diversity in addition to structural diversity. Behavioral diversity refers to the variety of search trajectories produced by different algorithms. Metrics such as nearest-neighbor distance, dispersion, and intensification ratio were used to characterize exploration and exploitation behaviors. Effective optimization required not only diversity but also the ability to adapt search behavior over time. LLMs introduced an advantage in generating conceptually diverse strategies. However, without adaptive control, this capability remained underutilized. 2.10 Research Gap and Synthesis In summary, the literature reveals three structural gaps in current LLM-driven Automated Algorithm Design: (1) the dominance of mutation-centric search, which lacks the theoretical grounding provided by the Building Block Hypothesis for complex optimization landscapes; (2) static operator scheduling that cannot adapt to the non-stationary reward dynamics inherent in evolutionary search; and (3) semantically weak recombination methods such as LMX that produce high rates of invalid offspring through syntactic code splicing. While individual solutions to these problems exist in isolation—Discounted Thompson Sampling for non-stationary bandits, generative crossover for semantic recombination, and normalized reward systems for meaningful feedback, no existing framework integrates all three into a unified evolutionary system. GA- LLaMEA is positioned to bridge these gaps by combining inspiration-based generative crossover with DTS adaptive operator control and per-operator normalized reward baselines, creating a framework capable of autonomous exploration-exploitation balancing throughout the evolutionary process. Each operator corresponds to a distinct exploration–exploitation behavior and is treated as an arm in the bandit framework. Refine Operator : The Refine operator performs targeted improvement of a single parent solution. A parent is selected from the current population, and its full source code is provided to the LLM with the instruction to refine the strategy of the algorithm. This operator focuses on improving optimization behavior, such as search dynamics, parameter adaptation, or exploration strategies, without explicitly enforcing structural simplification. It preserves the core structure of the parent while introducing strategic modifications. Simplify Operator : The Simplify operator performs refinement with an explicit focus on reducing algorithmic complexity. A single parent solution is selected from the current population, and its full source code is provided to the LLM along with the instruction to ``refine and simplify'' the algorithm. This operator aims to remove redundant logic, improve clarity, and reduce code bloat while preserving or improving performance. It primarily serves as an exploitation mechanism, producing more stable and robust solutions over time. Generative Crossover Operator : The Crossover operator generates new solutions by combining ideas from multiple existing algorithms through guided conceptual transfer. One high-performing parent is selected as the primary working algorithm, while additional solutions are provided as inspiration sources. The LLM is instructed to incorporate useful strategies, mechanisms, or structural ideas from the inspiration solutions into the working algorithm. This avoids direct code concatenation and instead promotes coherent integration at the conceptual level.This operator balances exploration and exploitation by leveraging high-quality solutions while introducing novel combinations. Random New Operator: The Random New operator generates entirely novel algorithms without relying on a specific parent. The LLM is provided with the task description and a summary of previously explored solutions, including their fitness scores. A structural template is provided to ensure syntactic correctness, but the model is explicitly instructed to produce a fundamentally different algorithm from those already explored. This operator serves as the primary exploration mechanism, enabling the discovery of new regions in the algorithm design space. This multi-run evaluation ensures statistical robustness and reduces the influence of stochastic variability inherent in both LLM generation and optimization processes. Such approaches highlight that, unlike traditional syntactic crossover, LLM-based recombination operates at a higher semantic level, enabling the synthesis of coherent and effective algorithmic strategies from multiple parents. Similar observations have been reported in recent LLM-based optimization frameworks, where multi-source inspiration improves both performance and robustness by enabling richer and more structured recombination (Li et al., 2025). Finally, the full GA- LLaMEA framework, which integrates multi-inspiration crossover with adaptive DTS operator selection, achieves a mean AOCC of 0.8348 with a standard deviation of 0.0255. While its mean performance is slightly below the fixed three-inspiration variant (Figure 11), it demonstrates improved stability, indicating that adaptive operator selection effectively balances exploration and exploitation while reducing variance across runs. This effect is also reflected in the tighter distribution observed in Figure 10 and is consistent with adaptive operator selection mechanisms that dynamically adjust search behavior based on observed rewards (Qi et al., 2023). By integrating Inspiration-Based Generative Crossover with Discounted Thompson Sampling for adaptive operator selection, the framework introduces both structural diversity and dynamic control into LLM-mediated optimization. This approach directly responds to recent calls for more structured and adaptive LLM-assisted evolutionary systems (van Stein et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). Empirical evaluation on the five-dimensional MA-BBOB benchmark demonstrated consistent and statistically stable improvements across competitive performance, convergence behavior, and structural integrity. GA- LLaMEA achieved the highest mean Elo rating among all compared frameworks, outperforming LLaMEA-Prompt5 and Evolution of Heuristics (Liu et al., 2024). More importantly, its superiority was not limited to final solution quality. Behavioral metrics revealed a stronger exploitation profile, including the lowest average distance-to-best and the highest intensification ratio, indicating effective local refinement once promising regions were identified. This aligns with established exploration–exploitation theory, which emphasizes dynamic balancing across optimization stages (Berger-Tal et al., 2014). Convergence analysis further showed that GA- LLaMEA produced sustained incremental improvements with minimal stagnation. The framework exhibited the highest success rate, the shortest longest no-improvement streak, and the lowest last-improvement fraction, demonstrating reliability throughout the optimization process rather than dependence on large sporadic improvement steps. These findings reinforce pattern-based perspectives in heuristic optimization, where adaptation and controlled intensification are essential design components ( Damasevicius , 2025). Structural analysis through Code Evolution Graphs provided additional insight into genealogical stability. Unlike mutation-dominant baselines that exhibited unstable complexity growth and token expansion, GA- LLaMEA maintained interconnected lineage structures and controlled code length. The guided conceptual transfer mechanism successfully prevented blind syntactic recombination, preserving semantic coherence while enabling strategic building-block synthesis. This directly addresses concerns regarding opacity and uncontrolled complexity in LLM-driven algorithm generation (van Stein et al., 2025; van Stein et al., 2025b). From a theoretical perspective, these findings align with established principles in evolutionary computation. Recombination facilitates efficient building-block assembly, while adaptive operator control is essential in non-stationary environments (Berger-Tal et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2024). Furthermore, integrating LLM reasoning into evolutionary frameworks reflects the broader paradigm of LLM-enhanced evolutionary computation (Wu et al., 2024) and LLM-guided evolutionary synthesis (Sadikov, 2025). Overall, GA- LLaMEA demonstrates that semantic-aware crossover combined with adaptive operator selection significantly enhances robustness, convergence reliability, and generalization performance in automated algorithm design. By coupling generative discovery with adaptive control and structural monitoring, the framework contributes toward the emerging vision of explainable and performance-aware automated algorithm design (van Stein et al., 2025b). Chapter VI RECOMMENDATION While GA- LLaMEA demonstrates significant improvements in automated algorithm design, future research must address dimensional scalability and architectural dependencies. The current framework was evaluated exclusively on the five-dimensional MA BBOB benchmark. Subsequent studies should expand testing to higher-dimensional search spaces to robustly assess the scalability of Inspiration-Based Generative Crossover and its ability to maintain convergence speed under increased complexity. Furthermore, as the present experimental setup relies on Gemini-2.5-Flash , integrating and benchmarking alternative state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) will clarify how varying foundational architectures and reasoning capabilities influence the success rate of generative recombination. The evaluation of the framework also highlights opportunities for controller refinement. To accommodate the highly restricted evaluation budgets of LLM optimization, the current system utilizes a discrete binary signal with a structural failure penalty. Future iterations could explore multi-objective reward structures that recognize partial algorithmic improvements without overwhelming the Discounted Thompson Sampling (DTS) mechanism. Additionally, because DTS relies on a predefined discount factor ( ) to control the memory decay rate of the bandit controller , investigating self-adaptive mechanisms for this parameter could further optimize the framework's ability to transition from exploration to exploitation dynamically in non-stationary environments. In light of the No Free Lunch theorems discussed in this study, a critical avenue for research is the application of the framework to generate domain-specific heuristics. While MA-BBOB provides a rigorous unbiased landscape, real-world optimization often requires algorithms tailored to specific structural constraints. Future studies should evaluate GA- LLaMEA's capacity to synthesize highly specialized operators for distinct problem classes , such as scheduling networks or complex data mining tasks, further establishing its versatility beyond generalized benchmark functions. Extending this framework to solve physical challenges in robotics, particularly within the context of deformable object manipulation, could validate its practical utility and adaptability in unpredictable, real-world environments.
